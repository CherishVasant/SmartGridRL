\title{Forecast-Driven Model Predictive Control with Reinforcement Learning for Low-Carbon Community Battery Scheduling}

\author{Elena, Margaret~Ellis, Anora~V and Cherish~Vasant}

\markboth{}{}

\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Home batteries can cut electricity bills and emissions by shifting consumption in time. But a controller in a real building
never knows the future demand, solar generation or carbon intensity. This paper proposes an \emph{RL-tuned
model predictive controller} for the batteries of a whole district. Gradient-boosted forecasters predict the load and
solar output of every building. A linear program then plans all batteries over the next 30 hours. A reinforcement learning (RL) agent, trained with
Truncated Quantile Critics (TQC), sets three weights of this program every hour: the cost of ramping, the price of
carbon and the value of stored energy. Its reward adds up to the official performance indicators and measures the gain
over leaving the batteries idle. The agent sees only district-level features, so one policy trained on five buildings
controls districts of any size. We evaluate it on the CityLearn Challenge 2022 benchmark with the official formulas, on
all 17 buildings and with three seeds. It lowers the electricity cost by 34.3\% and the emissions by 22.2\% on the
training buildings. Its Phase~III public score is $0.774 \pm 0.003$ and its private score $0.844 \pm 0.004$, which
places it 8th among the 42 public entries (winner 0.728). Its carbon score of 0.764 is the lowest of all 42 entries.
Ablations show that the optimiser, the forecasts and the RL tuning all contribute. The main remaining limit is the
forecast of the district's total load over the next few hours: a perfect forecast would lower the score to about 0.65.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\IEEEPARstart{B}{uildings} use a large share of all power. Rooftop photovoltaic (PV) panels and home batteries
now turn many of them into flexible grid resources. A battery that charges when power is cheap or clean, and discharges
when it is costly or carbon-intensive, lowers both the owner's bill and the emissions caused by the home. A fleet of
such batteries can also smooth the load that the local grid has to supply \cite{vazquez2019review,wang2020rlbuildings}.

Two families of controllers are common. Rule-based controllers are simple, but they cannot adapt to the weather, the
tariff or the state of the grid. Model predictive control (MPC) plans ahead with forecasts, but it is only as good as
those forecasts and as the fixed weights that trade cost against emissions and grid impact. Model-free deep
reinforcement learning (RL) \cite{sutton2018rl} learns a policy from interaction alone \cite{yu2021review,nagy2023tenquestions}.
On its own, an RL agent must discover the daily patterns of price and demand and the long-term effect of each
charge decision from a single scalar reward. The CityLearn environment \cite{vazquez2019citylearn,nweye2024citylearn} and
the NeurIPS 2022 CityLearn Challenge \cite{citylearn2022challenge} turned this problem into a shared benchmark. Its
official score combines the electricity cost, the carbon emissions and two grid key performance indicators (KPIs).

This paper proposes a controller in which each part does what it does best. Forecasters predict what can be predicted.
A linear program turns the forecasts into a battery schedule that respects the battery limits. An RL agent adjusts the
program's trade-offs every hour and corrects for what fixed weights and imperfect forecasts miss.

The contributions are as follows.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item An \emph{RL-tuned MPC} (Fig.~\ref{fig:arch}). A TQC agent sets three weights of a receding-horizon linear
        program every hour. Its inputs are district-level, so one policy runs on districts of any size.
  \item A \emph{score-aligned counterfactual reward}. Its terms add up to the official KPIs, use the no-battery
        run as their scale and measure the gain over idle batteries.
  \item A full evaluation. We score all 17 challenge homes with the official formulas over three seeds, compare
        with all 42 public leaderboard entries and the private medians, ablate each component, and show which forecast
        errors limit the score.
\end{enumerate}

Section~\ref{sec:related} reviews related work and Section~\ref{sec:problem} states the problem. Section~\ref{sec:method}
describes the controller and Section~\ref{sec:setup} the experiments. Section~\ref{sec:results} reports the results and
Section~\ref{sec:discussion} discusses them. Section~\ref{sec:sdg} relates the work to the Sustainable Development Goals,
and Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes.

\section{Related Work}\label{sec:related}

\subsection{Reinforcement Learning for Building Energy}
V\'azquez-Canteli and Nagy \cite{vazquez2019review} reviewed RL for demand response. Most of the studies they found used
simple function approximators, single homes and custom simulators, so their results were hard to compare. Wang and
Hong \cite{wang2020rlbuildings} and Yu \emph{et al.} \cite{yu2021review} surveyed deep RL for building control. They
named sample efficiency, safety, reward design and transfer across homes as the main open problems. Nagy \emph{et
al.} \cite{nagy2023tenquestions} raised ten questions on RL for building energy, including the need for shared
benchmarks and KPIs. Early deep RL work controlled heating and cooling with deep Q-networks
\cite{wei2017hvac,mnih2015dqn} and optimised building energy online with policy-gradient and Q-learning methods
\cite{mocanu2019online}. Dulac-Arnold \emph{et al.} \cite{dulacarnold2021challenges} listed the challenges of real-world
RL. Several of them, such as multi-objective rewards, partial observability and limited data, are central to battery
control.

\subsection{CityLearn and Grid-Interactive Communities}
CityLearn \cite{vazquez2019citylearn} is an OpenAI Gym environment for demand response in districts of homes with
storage and PV. Its second version \cite{nweye2024citylearn} adds electric vehicles, occupant comfort, resilience and
carbon-aware control. MARLISA \cite{vazquez2020marlisa} coordinated several agents through sequential action selection
to shape the district load. Nweye \emph{et al.} \cite{nweye2022realworld} studied the real-world challenges of
multi-agent RL in such communities, and MERLIN \cite{nweye2023merlin} used offline and transfer learning for them. The
NeurIPS 2022 CityLearn Challenge \cite{citylearn2022challenge} scored controllers on cost, carbon and grid KPIs, each
divided by its value with no batteries. We use its data, its KPIs and its leaderboards as references.

\subsection{Forecasting, Optimisation and the Challenge Winners}
MPC solves an optimisation problem over a moving horizon of forecasts and applies only the first decision
\cite{rawlings2017mpc}. The organisers' overview \cite{nweye2023challenge} shows that all top entries of the 2022
challenge combined forecasting with optimisation or learning. The winner, Together, forecast load and PV with
gradient-boosted trees and linear models. It then mixed a rolling-horizon stochastic optimiser with multi-agent PPO
\cite{yu2022mappo}. The runner-up used dynamic programming in the first phase and, later, forecasts with a small policy
network trained by CMA-ES \cite{hansen2016cma}. The third team, CUFE, solved a 24-hour linear program per building with
linear forecasts. The fourth, DivMARL, averaged a shared SAC policy with a rule driven by XGBoost forecasts. Our
forecasters use LightGBM \cite{ke2017lightgbm} with settings chosen by Optuna \cite{akiba2019optuna}.

\subsection{Deep RL for Continuous Control}
The MPC weights are continuous, so actor-critic methods fit the task. DDPG \cite{lillicrap2016ddpg} and TD3
\cite{fujimoto2018td3} learn deterministic policies with off-policy critics, and TD3 adds clipped double Q-learning to
limit over-estimation. Soft Actor-Critic (SAC) \cite{haarnoja2018sac,haarnoja2018sacapps} maximises the reward plus the
policy entropy and tunes its temperature automatically. It is among the most robust off-policy methods. PPO
\cite{schulman2017ppo} is a common on-policy alternative. TQC \cite{kuznetsov2020tqc}, which we use, extends SAC with a
mixture of distributional critics and drops their largest quantiles to reduce over-estimation. REDQ \cite{chen2021redq}
and CrossQ \cite{bhatt2024crossq} pursue the same aim with critic ensembles and batch normalisation, and multi-step
returns \cite{hessel2018rainbow} pass delayed rewards back faster. We use the Stable-Baselines3 implementations
\cite{raffin2021sb3}.

\subsection{Reward Design}
Battery control has several objectives \cite{roijers2013survey}. Cost, emissions, ramping and peaks often conflict, and a
single reward needs weights and scales for them. If the scales are poor, one objective dominates the others. In
multi-task learning, such weights are often adapted during training \cite{liu2019dwa,kendall2018uncertainty}. Ng
\emph{et al.} \cite{ng1999shaping} showed which changes to a reward leave the optimal policy unchanged. Our counterfactual
baseline relies on this result. For storage arbitrage, Cao \emph{et al.} \cite{cao2020arbitrage} showed that a
degradation model in the reward changes the learned strategy. This motivates the small battery-wear term in our reward.

\subsection{Evaluation of RL Results}
Henderson \emph{et al.} \cite{henderson2018matters} and Agarwal \emph{et al.} \cite{agarwal2021precipice} showed that
deep RL results vary strongly with the random seed, so single runs are unreliable. We thus report the proposed
controller as the mean and standard deviation of three seeds. Each seed is evaluated without exploration over a full year.

\section{Problem Formulation}\label{sec:problem}

\subsection{Environment}
We use CityLearn 2.1.2 with the data of the 2022 challenge. It contains 17 homes in three districts: five public
training buildings (1--5), five validation buildings (6--10) and seven test buildings (11--17). Each home has a load it
cannot shift, rooftop PV and one battery of 6.4\,kWh and 5\,kW. The simulation runs hourly for one year, or 8{,}759
control steps. Every hour, the controller sets the charge or discharge rate of each battery as a fraction of its
capacity. The decision acts over the following hour. Power is billed with a time-of-use tariff, and the carbon
intensity of the grid changes over the day.

\subsection{Information Available to the Controller}
At each hour the controller sees the month, the day type and the hour. It also sees the outdoor temperature, the
humidity and the diffuse and direct solar irradiance, each with the environment's 6, 12 and 24-hour forecasts, and the
current carbon intensity. For each home it sees the load, the PV output, the battery state of charge (SoC), the net
consumption, and the current price with its 6, 12 and 24-hour values from the tariff.

\subsection{Key Performance Indicators}
Let $E_t$ be the district's net consumption at hour $t$ (positive for imports), $\pi_t$ the price and $I_t$ the carbon
intensity. A superscript ``nb'' marks the same quantity in the run without batteries. The official KPIs are
\begin{align}
  C &= \frac{\sum_t \max(0, E_t \pi_t)}{\sum_t \max(0, E_t^{\mathrm{nb}} \pi_t)}, \qquad
  G = \frac{\sum_t \max(0, E_t I_t)}{\sum_t \max(0, E_t^{\mathrm{nb}} I_t)}, \\
  R &= \frac{\sum_t |E_t - E_{t-1}|}{\sum_t |E_t^{\mathrm{nb}} - E_{t-1}^{\mathrm{nb}}|}, \qquad
  D = \tfrac{1}{2}\bigl(R + (1-L)\bigr),
\end{align}
where $C$ is the cost, $G$ the carbon emissions, $R$ the ramping and $1-L$ the mean monthly value of $1 - \bar E / \max E$
relative to no battery. The grid KPI $D$ averages the last two. Exports earn nothing, and the clipping applies to
the district total, so one home's export offsets another's import. All KPIs are ratios to the no-battery run,
so values below one are gains. The score of a district $d$ is $S_d=(C+G+D)/3$. The first phase of the challenge ranked
entries by $(C+G)/2$ on the training buildings, which we call the Phase~I score. The final phase ranked them by a
\emph{public} score $0.4\,S_\mathrm{train} + 0.6\,S_\mathrm{val}$. It chose the winners by a \emph{private} score
$0.2\,S_\mathrm{train} + 0.3\,S_\mathrm{val} + 0.5\,S_\mathrm{test}$ \cite{nweye2023challenge}.

Fig.~\ref{fig:arch} shows the controller. Every hour it runs four steps. It forecasts the next $H = 30$ hours. The RL
agent then sets three weights from a small district-level state. A linear program, which we call the solver, uses these
weights to plan all batteries over the horizon. Only the first hour of the plan is applied, and the cycle repeats an hour later. The
subsections below describe the three parts.

\subsection{Forecasting Module}\label{sec:fc}
Each hour the controller forecasts every home's load and PV output, the carbon intensity and the price for the next
$H$ hours. One LightGBM model \cite{ke2017lightgbm} per quantity covers all horizons
and homes. Its inputs are recent and lagged values, the mean of the same hour over the past seven days, the calendar,
the current weather and the environment's weather forecast for the target hour. Each value input is divided by the
home's mean absolute value over the past week. A model trained on some homes thus applies to others.

The model for a district is trained on the homes of the other two districts, and its settings are chosen with Optuna
\cite{akiba2019optuna} by cross-validation between them. Carbon intensity and price follow simple seasonal rules. The carbon forecast averages the same hour yesterday and over the last
week and adds the current error, which decays as $0.8^h$ over the horizon. The price forecast repeats the tariff of the
same hour one week earlier.

\subsection{Receding-Horizon Linear Program}\label{sec:mpc}
The MPC receives the forecast net load of the district $\hat B_h$ (load minus PV), the prices $\hat\pi_h$ and the carbon
intensities $\hat I_h$. It then solves
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
\min\; & \sum_{h=1}^{H} \Bigl(\tfrac{\hat\pi_h}{\bar c} + w_g \tfrac{\hat I_h}{\bar g}\Bigr) m_h
        + \tfrac{w_r}{2\bar r} \sum_{h=1}^{H} \rho_h - v\,\eta \sum_b s_{b,H} \\
\text{s.t. } & m_h \ge \hat B_h + \textstyle\sum_b (x^+_{b,h} - x^-_{b,h}),\; m_h \ge 0, \\
& \rho_h \ge |E_h - E_{h-1}|,\; E_h = \hat B_h + \textstyle\sum_b (x^+_{b,h} - x^-_{b,h}), \\
& s_{b,h} = s_{b,h-1} + \eta x^+_{b,h} - x^-_{b,h}/\eta,\; 0 \le s_{b,h} \le 6.4, \\
& 0 \le x^\pm_{b,h} \le \min(5,\ 21 - 3.125\, s_{b,h-1}),
\end{split}
\label{eq:lp}
\end{equation}
where $m_h$ is the district import, $\rho_h$ the ramp and $s_{b,h}$ the energy stored in battery $b$. The charge and
discharge powers are $x^\pm_{b,h}$, with an efficiency of $\eta=\sqrt{0.9}$ each way. The last constraint models
CityLearn's power taper above 80\% charge. The running no-battery means $\bar c$, $\bar g$ and $\bar r$ put cost, carbon
and ramping on the scale of the KPIs. The weight $v$ is the median unit cost over the horizon and gives a value to
the energy left at its end. Exports earn nothing, so only imports are priced.

Only the first hour of the plan is applied. It moves each battery to its planned state of charge, and one
hour later it solves the problem again with new forecasts (Fig.~\ref{fig:horizon}). The HiGHS solver needs about
20\,ms per problem.

\subsection{RL Weight Tuner}\label{sec:tuner}
Fixed weights in (\ref{eq:lp}) cannot adapt to the season, the forecast quality or the battery state. The RL agent
thus outputs three numbers $a_t \in [-1,1]^3$ every hour. They set the ramp weight $w_r = 2\cdot 4^{a_1}$, the
carbon weight $w_g = 2^{a_2}$ and the end-of-horizon value $v(1 + a_3/2)$. Each weight is centred on a good fixed
setting, so an action of zero gives a sound controller. The agent's state is district-level. It holds the forecasts of
net load, carbon intensity and price over the horizon, and the time of day and season. It also holds the mean state of
charge, the current load and month peak, and the latest forecast error. None of these depends on the number of homes. A policy trained on
the five training buildings thus also controls the seven test buildings.

\emph{Score-aligned counterfactual reward.} The reward is built so that its terms add up exactly to the numerators of
the official KPIs, on their scale. At hour $t$ we use the district's clipped cost and emissions and its ramp,
\begin{equation}
\begin{gathered}
  \hat c_t = \frac{\max(0, E_t\pi_t)}{\bar c^{\,\mathrm{nb}}}, \qquad
  \hat g_t = \frac{\max(0, E_t I_t)}{\bar g^{\,\mathrm{nb}}}, \\
  \hat r_t = \frac{|E_t - E_{t-1}|}{\bar r^{\,\mathrm{nb}}},
\end{gathered}
\end{equation}
where a bar marks the mean of the term per step in the no-battery run. Then $\sum_t \hat c_t = T\cdot C$, so the return
over a year is a linear function of the KPIs. The peak term $\hat p_t$ is the rise of the running daily
maximum of $E_t$. The first hour of each day counts its full load, so the terms of a day add up to that day's peak. The
wear term is $\hat b_t = \sum_i |\mathrm{SoC}_{i,t}-\mathrm{SoC}_{i,t-1}|$. The reward is
\begin{equation}
  \begin{split}
  r_t = &-\bigl(\hat c_t + w_g\hat g_t + w_r\hat r_t + w_p\hat p_t + w_b\hat b_t\bigr) \\
        &+ \bigl(\hat c^{\,\mathrm{nb}}_t + w_g\hat g^{\,\mathrm{nb}}_t + w_r\hat r^{\,\mathrm{nb}}_t + w_p\hat p^{\,\mathrm{nb}}_t\bigr).
  \end{split}
  \label{eq:reward}
\end{equation}
The second bracket holds the same terms for the case in which the batteries stayed idle in that hour. It depends only on
outside data, not on the actions or the battery state. It thus leaves the optimal policy unchanged
\cite{ng1999shaping}, but it removes most of the variation caused by demand. The agent is rewarded for the
\emph{improvement} it causes, and a reward of zero means ``as good as no battery''. A real system can compute this
baseline online from the measured net load and the battery's own power. The weights follow the score
$S=(C+G+(R+1-L)/2)/3$. Cost has weight 1, ramping and peak have $w_r = w_p = 0.5$, and wear has $w_b=0.1$. The peak term
stands in for the load factor at each step. A carbon weight of $w_g = 2$ puts extra weight on emissions.

\emph{Algorithm.} We train the agent with TQC \cite{kuznetsov2020tqc}. It has an actor and two distributional critics
with 25 quantiles each. The two largest quantiles of each critic are dropped when the target is formed. This limits the
over-estimation that a noisy, long-horizon reward invites. Training uses the training district only. At evaluation the
actor acts deterministically.

\section{Experimental Setup}\label{sec:setup}
The tuner is trained for 100{,}000 environment steps, about 11.4 simulated years, on the five training buildings. The
learning rate is $3\cdot10^{-4}$, the replay buffer holds 200{,}000 transitions and the batch size is 256. We use
$\tau=0.005$, $\gamma=0.99$, one gradient step per environment step, 1{,}000 warm-up steps and automatic entropy tuning.
The actor and each critic have two hidden layers of 64 units. We run three seeds (42, 100 and 2026) and report the mean
and standard deviation.

\emph{Evaluation.} The trained policy runs without retraining on each district for the full year. The KPIs are computed against the no-battery run on the same homes.
CityLearn 2.1.2 contains all 17 homes, so we compute the public and private scores ourselves with the official
formulas. These are not the scores of the challenge's own evaluator. The references are no battery, where every
KPI equals one, and the rule-based controller (RBC) supplied with CityLearn.

\emph{Ablations.} Each ablation changes one part of the controller and keeps the same protocol. We replace the RL tuner
with the best fixed weights we found (ramp weight 3, carbon weight 1, 24-hour horizon). We try horizons of 24 and
36 hours. We replace the tuned LightGBM settings with the defaults, and the LightGBM load and PV forecasts with seasonal
ones. We use SAC instead of TQC. Last, we remove the solver: a TQC agent with the same reward then sets the battery
rates directly, from the observations plus past-only history features, over 200{,}000 steps. Ablations with one seed use
seed 42.

\section{Results}\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Main Results}
The controller improves every KPI in every district (Table~\ref{tab:main}, Fig.~\ref{fig:kpis}). On the training
homes it lowers the cost by 34.3\% and the emissions by 22.2\% relative to no battery. It also smooths the district
load, with $R = 0.823$, $1-L = 0.977$ and $D = 0.900$. The RBC, in contrast, is worse than idle batteries on every
KPI. It charges and discharges on a fixed schedule that ignores the tariff, the carbon intensity and the PV.

The gains carry over to the validation and test buildings. On the validation buildings the cost falls by 34.5\%
and the emissions by 24.5\%. The scores of the training and validation districts are almost equal ($S = 0.778$ and
0.771), so the controller is not fitted to its training buildings. The seven test buildings differ most from the
training ones and gain less ($S = 0.914$). There the cost falls by 19.9\% and the emissions by 4.1\%. The three seeds
agree closely, with a standard deviation of at most 0.006 for every score.

\subsection{Learned Behaviour}
Fig.~\ref{fig:daily} shows the average day on the training buildings. With no batteries, the district exports up to
5\,kW of PV at midday for nothing and imports about 6\,kW in the evening. The controller stores the midday surplus. The
mean state of charge rises from under 10\% at dawn to about 87\% at 14:00. The batteries then discharge through the peak
tariff from 16:00 to 21:00 and into the night. The net load stays between about 0 and 4.5\,kW across the day, so the
grid sees a much flatter profile.

Fig.~\ref{fig:week} shows one July week in detail. The daily pattern repeats, but the plot also reveals the
controller's weak point. On one day it tops up from the grid in the last cheap hour before the peak tariff, which

With the reward of (\ref{eq:reward}), the mean reward per step is the weighted gain over idle batteries
(Fig.~\ref{fig:learning}). The tuner starts from the weights of a good fixed MPC. Even the first, partly random year
thus earns 0.84 per step, far above zero. Learning raises the reward by about 4\% within five simulated years, and
it then stays flat. One seed (2026) levels off at a lower training reward. Its public score (0.777) still lies within
one standard deviation of the others. Once the solver handles the schedule, the remaining differences in the weights
matter little.

\subsection{Ablations}
Table~\ref{tab:ablation} changes one component at a time.

\emph{The solver matters most.} Without it, an RL agent with the same reward sets the battery rates directly. Even
with history features and twice the training, it reaches only $S = 0.862$ on the training buildings and a public score of
0.854. It must learn from the reward the daily structure that the forecasts and the linear program give explicitly, and
it ramps more than idle batteries ($D = 1.06$).

\emph{The RL tuning helps.} With the best fixed weights, the same solver scores 0.781 (public) and 0.846 (private).
The tuner lowers these to 0.774 and 0.844, and the Phase~I score from 0.742 to 0.717. Most of the gain comes from cost,
which falls from 0.703 to 0.657 on the training buildings, at the price of slightly more ramping. In effect, the tuner
learns when the forecasts justify a more aggressive arbitrage.

\emph{Good forecasts are needed.} With seasonal load and PV forecasts and a 24-hour horizon, the tuned controller loses
most of its edge on new homes (public 0.807, private 0.864). It still does well on the training buildings
(Phase~I 0.728). With poor forecasts, the agent learns weights that suit only the homes it was trained on. Tuning
the LightGBM settings raises the PV accuracy from about 90\% to 96\%. At a 24-hour horizon it lowers the public score
from 0.782 to 0.774.

\emph{Other choices matter little.} Horizons of 24, 30 and 36 hours give public scores within 0.002 of each other. SAC
instead of TQC gives $0.775 \pm 0.001$. The result thus depends on the architecture rather than on these settings.

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}
\emph{Why RL works best as a tuner.} The agent does not have to discover the daily patterns of price, PV and demand, or
the physics of the batteries. The forecasts and the linear program supply both, and the agent's three actions only shift
a schedule that is already sensible. A credit-assignment problem over thousands of hourly battery decisions becomes a
much easier one. This explains why removing the solver costs far more (public 0.854) than removing the tuner (0.781).
The tuning pays off only with good forecasts. With seasonal forecasts the agent fits its weights to the
training buildings.

\emph{Grid KPIs.} The grid KPI ($D$ 0.902 on the public blend) is the main gap to the winner (0.738). Short
import spikes, such as a top-up in the last cheap hour before the peak tariff (Fig.~\ref{fig:week}), are cheap in cost
and carbon but costly in ramping and load factor. A load-factor term in the linear program is one remedy. Better
forecasts of the district total, the quantity that matters most (Section~\ref{sec:fcvalue}), are the other.

\emph{Transfer.} The controller runs on new districts of a different size without retraining. It could thus serve as a
starting point that is fine-tuned on site.

\emph{Limitations.} The study covers one climate and one year of data, with five training buildings. The environment
does not forecast carbon intensity or load. We compute the scores with the official formulas on CityLearn 2.1.2, not with
the challenge's evaluator. The weather ``forecasts'' of the data equal the actual weather, but every entry received the
same data. Three seeds give reliable means for the proposed controller, with standard deviations of at most 0.006, but
most ablations use one seed. Hourly household load remains hard to forecast. Better inputs, such as occupancy, appliance
schedules or sub-hourly data, are more likely to help than larger forecasting models.

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}
We proposed an RL-tuned model predictive controller for community battery storage. LightGBM forecasts drive
a receding-horizon linear program, and a TQC agent retunes its ramping, carbon and storage weights every hour from
district-level features. The agent learns from a reward that adds up to the KPIs. On the CityLearn 2022
benchmark the controller cut the electricity cost by 34.3\% and the emissions by 22.2\% on the training buildings. Over
three seeds it reached a public score of $0.774 \pm 0.003$ and a private score of $0.844 \pm 0.004$, between 7th and 8th
place on the public leaderboard. Its carbon score of 0.764 is the lowest of all 42 entries (best 0.803), and every
private KPI beats the participants' median.

The ablations show that the solver contributes most and that the RL tuning adds a steady gain once the forecasts are
good. The result is robust to the horizon and to the choice of RL algorithm. The remaining gap to the winner lies in the
grid KPI and, above all, in the forecast of the district's total load over the next few hours. With perfect load
forecasts, the same solver would score about 0.65. For practitioners, the lesson is to align the reward with the
evaluation, to use RL to adapt an optimiser rather than to replace it, and to invest in forecasting.
