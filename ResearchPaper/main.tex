\begin{abstract}
Residential batteries can reduce electricity costs and emissions by shifting consumption in time, but a controller in a
real building cannot observe future demand, solar generation or carbon intensity. This work proposes an RL-tuned model
predictive controller for the batteries of a whole district. Gradient-boosted forecasters predict the load and solar
output of every building, and a linear program uses these forecasts to plan all batteries over the next 30 hours. A
reinforcement learning (RL) agent, trained with Truncated Quantile Critics (TQC) and a reward that adds up to the
official performance indicators, sets three weights of this program every hour from district-level features. On the
CityLearn Challenge 2022 benchmark, with 17 buildings and three seeds, the controller lowers the electricity cost by
34.3 percent and the emissions by 22.2 percent in the training district. Its mean Phase III public score is 0.774 and
its private score 0.844, which places it 8th among the 42 public entries, and its carbon score of 0.764 is the lowest
of all 42 entries. Our ablations show that the optimiser, the forecasts and the RL tuning all contribute.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Buildings use a large share of all electricity, and rooftop photovoltaic (PV) panels and home
batteries now turn many of them into flexible grid resources. Batteries that charge in low-price or low-carbon hours and
discharge in high-price or carbon-intensive hours reduce both cost and emissions, and across a district they can smooth
the aggregate load seen by the grid \cite{vazquez2019review,wang2020rlbuildings}. The resulting control problem is
challenging, because each action changes the state of charge and its effect extends many hours into the future, which
makes credit assignment difficult.

Rule-based controllers are simple, but they cannot adapt to the weather, the tariff or the state of the grid. Model
predictive control (MPC) plans ahead with forecasts, but its performance depends on the accuracy of its forecasts and on
fixed weights that trade cost against emissions and grid impact. Model-free deep reinforcement learning (RL)
\cite{sutton2018rl} learns a policy from interaction alone \cite{yu2021review,nagy2023tenquestions}, but on its own it
must discover the daily patterns of price and demand, and the long-term effect of each charge decision, from a scalar
reward. The CityLearn environment \cite{vazquez2019citylearn,nweye2024citylearn} and the NeurIPS 2022 CityLearn
Challenge \cite{citylearn2022challenge} turned this problem into a shared benchmark, whose score combines the
electricity cost, the carbon emissions and two grid key performance indicators (KPIs).

This work combines the two ideas in an \emph{RL-tuned MPC} (Fig.~\ref{fig:arch}). Gradient-boosted models forecast the
load and PV of each building, a linear program turns these forecasts into a battery schedule that respects the battery
limits, and a TQC agent sets three weights of the program every hour. Since its inputs are district-level, one policy
runs on districts of any size. Our second contribution is a \emph{score-aligned counterfactual reward}, whose terms add
up to the official KPIs, use the no-battery run as their scale and measure the gain over idle batteries. Third, we
evaluate the controller on all 17 challenge buildings with the official formulas over three seeds, compare it with all
42 public leaderboard entries and the private medians, ablate each component and show which forecast errors limit the
score.

\section{Literature Review}\label{sec:related}

\subsection{RL for Building Energy and CityLearn}
V\'azquez-Canteli and Nagy \cite{vazquez2019review} found that most RL studies for demand response used simple function
approximators, single buildings and custom simulators, which made their results difficult to compare. Later surveys
\cite{wang2020rlbuildings,yu2021review} named sample efficiency, safety, reward design and transfer across buildings as
the main open problems, and Nagy \emph{et al.} \cite{nagy2023tenquestions} stressed the need for shared benchmarks and
KPIs. Dulac-Arnold \emph{et al.} \cite{dulacarnold2021challenges} listed the challenges of real-world RL, of which
multi-objective rewards, partial observability and limited data are central to battery control. CityLearn
\cite{vazquez2019citylearn,nweye2024citylearn} provides districts of buildings with storage and PV. MARLISA
\cite{vazquez2020marlisa} coordinated several agents through sequential action selection, and Nweye \emph{et al.}
\cite{nweye2022realworld} studied the real-world challenges of multi-agent RL in such communities.

\subsection{Forecast-Driven Optimisation in the CityLearn Challenge}
MPC solves an optimisation problem over a moving horizon of forecasts and applies only the first decision
\cite{rawlings2017mpc}. According to the organisers' overview \cite{nweye2023challenge}, all top entries of the 2022
challenge combined forecasting with optimisation or learning. The winner, Together, forecast load and PV with
gradient-boosted trees and linear models and mixed a rolling-horizon stochastic optimiser with multi-agent PPO. The runner-up used dynamic programming in the first phase and, later, a small policy network trained
by CMA-ES. CUFE solved a 24-hour linear program per building, while DivMARL averaged a shared SAC
policy with a rule driven by XGBoost forecasts.

\subsection{Deep RL Algorithms and Reward Design}
The MPC weights are continuous, so actor-critic methods are a natural choice. DDPG \cite{lillicrap2016ddpg} and TD3
\cite{fujimoto2018td3} learn deterministic policies with off-policy critics, SAC \cite{haarnoja2018sac} adds an entropy
term with automatic temperature tuning, and PPO \cite{schulman2017ppo} is a common on-policy alternative. We use TQC
\cite{kuznetsov2020tqc}, which extends SAC with distributional critics whose largest quantiles are dropped to reduce
over-estimation, as implemented in Stable-Baselines3 \cite{raffin2021sb3}. Battery control has several conflicting
objectives, so a single reward needs weights and scales for them. Ng \emph{et al.}
\cite{ng1999shaping} showed which reward transformations leave the optimal policy unchanged, which our counterfactual
baseline relies on, and Cao \emph{et al.} \cite{cao2020arbitrage} showed that a degradation model in the reward changes
the learned arbitrage strategy. Because deep RL results vary strongly with the random seed
\cite{henderson2018matters,agarwal2021precipice}, we report the mean and standard deviation of three seeds.

\section{Problem Formulation}\label{sec:problem}

\subsection{Simulation Environment and Observations}
We use CityLearn 2.1.2 with the data of the 2022 challenge, which contains 17 buildings in three districts: five public
training buildings (1--5), five validation buildings (6--10) and seven test buildings (11--17). Each building has a load
it cannot shift, rooftop PV and one battery of 6.4\,kWh and 5\,kW. The simulation runs hourly for one year (8{,}759
steps), and every hour the controller sets the charge or discharge rate of each battery. Electricity is billed under a
time-of-use tariff, and the carbon intensity of the grid varies over the day. The controller observes the calendar, the
weather with the environment's 6, 12 and 24-hour forecasts, the carbon intensity, the price with its tariff values, and
the load, PV output, state of charge (SoC) and net consumption of each building.

\subsection{Key Performance Indicators}
Let $E_t$ be the district's net consumption at hour $t$ (positive for imports), $\pi_t$ the price and $I_t$ the carbon
intensity. We mark the same quantity in the run without batteries with a superscript ``nb''. The official KPIs are
\begin{align}
  C &= \frac{\sum_t \max(0, E_t \pi_t)}{\sum_t \max(0, E_t^{\mathrm{nb}} \pi_t)}, \qquad
  G = \frac{\sum_t \max(0, E_t I_t)}{\sum_t \max(0, E_t^{\mathrm{nb}} I_t)}, \\
  R &= \frac{\sum_t |E_t - E_{t-1}|}{\sum_t |E_t^{\mathrm{nb}} - E_{t-1}^{\mathrm{nb}}|}, \qquad
  D = \tfrac{1}{2}\bigl(R + (1-L)\bigr),
\end{align}
where $C$ is the cost, $G$ the carbon emissions, $R$ the ramping and $1-L$ the mean monthly value of $1 - \bar E / \max E$
relative to no battery. Exports are not paid, and since the clipping applies to the district total, one building's
export can offset another's import. All KPIs are ratios to the no-battery run, so values below one are gains.

The score of a district $d$ is $S_d=(C+G+D)/3$. The first phase of the challenge ranked entries by $(C+G)/2$ on the
training buildings, which we call the Phase~I score. The final phase ranked them by a \emph{public} score
$0.4\,S_\mathrm{train} + 0.6\,S_\mathrm{val}$, and it chose the winners by a \emph{private} score
$0.2\,S_\mathrm{train} + 0.3\,S_\mathrm{val} + 0.5\,S_\mathrm{test}$ \cite{nweye2023challenge}. The public score is our main measure.

Every hour, the controller in Fig.~\ref{fig:arch} forecasts the next $H = 30$ hours, the RL agent sets three weights
from a district-level state, and a linear program, which we call the solver, uses these weights to plan all batteries
over the horizon.

\subsection{Forecasting Module}\label{sec:fc}
Each hour we forecast every building's load and PV output, the carbon intensity and the price for the next $H$ hours. One
LightGBM model \cite{ke2017lightgbm} per quantity covers all horizons and buildings. Its inputs are recent and lagged
values, the mean of the same hour over the past seven days, the calendar, the current weather and the environment's
weather forecast for the target hour. We divide each value input by the building's mean absolute value over the past week,
so that a model trained on some buildings also applies to others. The model for a district is trained on the buildings of the
other two districts, and Optuna \cite{akiba2019optuna} chooses its settings by cross-validation between them. Carbon
intensity is forecast as the mean of the same hour yesterday and over the last week plus the current error, decaying as
$0.8^h$, and the price as the tariff of the same hour one week earlier.

\subsection{Receding-Horizon Linear Program}\label{sec:mpc}
Our MPC receives the forecast net load of the district $\hat B_h$ (load minus PV), the prices $\hat\pi_h$ and the carbon
intensities $\hat I_h$. It then solves
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
\min\; & \sum_{h=1}^{H} \Bigl(\tfrac{\hat\pi_h}{\bar c} + w_g \tfrac{\hat I_h}{\bar g}\Bigr) m_h
        + \tfrac{w_r}{2\bar r} \sum_{h=1}^{H} \rho_h - v\,\eta \sum_b s_{b,H} \\
\text{s.t. } & m_h \ge \hat B_h + \textstyle\sum_b (x^+_{b,h} - x^-_{b,h}),\; m_h \ge 0, \\
& \rho_h \ge |E_h - E_{h-1}|,\; E_h = \hat B_h + \textstyle\sum_b (x^+_{b,h} - x^-_{b,h}), \\
& s_{b,h} = s_{b,h-1} + \eta x^+_{b,h} - x^-_{b,h}/\eta,\; 0 \le s_{b,h} \le 6.4, \\
& 0 \le x^\pm_{b,h} \le \min(5,\ 21 - 3.125\, s_{b,h-1}),
\end{split}
\label{eq:lp}
\end{equation}
where $m_h$ is the district import (exports are not paid), $\rho_h$ the ramp, $s_{b,h}$ the energy stored in battery
$b$, and $x^\pm_{b,h}$ the charge and discharge powers, limited to 5\,kW, with an efficiency of $\eta=\sqrt{0.9}$ each
way. The last constraint models CityLearn's power taper above 80\% charge. The running no-battery means $\bar c$,
$\bar g$ and $\bar r$ put cost, carbon and ramping on the scale of the KPIs, and $v$, the median unit cost over the
horizon, values the energy left at its end. Only the first hour of the plan is applied, and the problem is solved
again one hour later with new forecasts (Fig.~\ref{fig:horizon}). With the HiGHS solver, each problem takes about
20\,ms.

\subsection{Reinforcement Learning Weight Tuner}\label{sec:tuner}
Fixed weights in (\ref{eq:lp}) cannot adapt to the season, the forecast quality or the battery state, so an RL
agent outputs three numbers $a_t \in [-1,1]^3$ every hour. They set the ramp weight $w_r = 2\cdot 4^{a_1}$, the
carbon weight $w_g = 2^{a_2}$ and the end-of-horizon value $v(1 + a_3/2)$. Each weight is centred on a good fixed
setting. A zero action thus reproduces a fixed-weight MPC. The agent's state holds the forecasts of net load,
carbon intensity and price over the horizon, the time of day and season, the mean state of charge, the current load
and month peak, and the latest forecast error. None of these depends on the number of buildings. A policy trained on five
buildings can therefore control the seven test buildings as well.

The agent learns from a \emph{score-aligned counterfactual reward}, built so that its terms add up exactly to the
numerators of the official KPIs, on their scale. At hour $t$ we use the district's clipped cost and emissions and its ramp,
\begin{equation}
\begin{gathered}
  \hat c_t = \frac{\max(0, E_t\pi_t)}{\bar c^{\,\mathrm{nb}}}, \qquad
  \hat g_t = \frac{\max(0, E_t I_t)}{\bar g^{\,\mathrm{nb}}}, \\
  \hat r_t = \frac{|E_t - E_{t-1}|}{\bar r^{\,\mathrm{nb}}},
\end{gathered}
\end{equation}
where a bar marks the mean of the term per step in the no-battery run. Then $\sum_t \hat c_t = T\cdot C$, so the return
over a year is a linear function of the KPIs. The peak term $\hat p_t$ is the rise of the running daily
maximum of $E_t$. The first hour of each day counts its full load, so the terms of a day add up to that day's peak. The
wear term is $\hat b_t = \sum_i |\mathrm{SoC}_{i,t}-\mathrm{SoC}_{i,t-1}|$. The reward is
\begin{equation}
  \begin{split}
  r_t = &-\bigl(\hat c_t + w_g\hat g_t + w_r\hat r_t + w_p\hat p_t + w_b\hat b_t\bigr) \\
        &+ \bigl(\hat c^{\,\mathrm{nb}}_t + w_g\hat g^{\,\mathrm{nb}}_t + w_r\hat r^{\,\mathrm{nb}}_t + w_p\hat p^{\,\mathrm{nb}}_t\bigr).
  \end{split}
  \label{eq:reward}
\end{equation}
The second bracket holds the same terms for the case in which the batteries stayed idle in that hour. Since it depends
only on outside data, and not on the actions or the battery state, it leaves the optimal policy unchanged
\cite{ng1999shaping}. It does, however, remove most of the variation caused by demand. The agent is thus rewarded for
the \emph{improvement} it causes, and a reward of zero means ``as good as no battery''. A real system can compute this
baseline online from the measured net load and the battery's own power.

The weights follow the score $S=(C+G+(R+1-L)/2)/3$: cost has weight 1, ramping and peak $w_r = w_p = 0.5$ (the peak
term stands in for the load factor), wear $w_b=0.1$, and carbon $w_g = 2$, which puts extra weight on emissions. We
train the agent with TQC \cite{kuznetsov2020tqc}, using an actor and two distributional critics with 25 quantiles each,
of which the two largest are dropped when the target is formed. This limits the over-estimation that a noisy,
long-horizon reward invites.

\section{Experimental Setup}\label{sec:setup}
We train the tuner for 100{,}000 environment steps, about 11.4 simulated years, on the five training buildings, with a
learning rate of $3\cdot10^{-4}$, a replay buffer of 200{,}000 transitions and a batch size of 256. We use
$\tau=0.005$, $\gamma=0.99$, one gradient step per environment step, 1{,}000 warm-up steps and automatic entropy tuning.
The actor and each critic have two hidden layers of 64 units, and we run three seeds (42, 100 and 2026) and report
the mean and standard deviation.

The trained policy then runs deterministically on each district for the full year without retraining. Because
CityLearn 2.1.2 contains all 17 buildings, we compute the KPIs and the public and private scores ourselves with the
official formulas, not with the challenge's evaluator. As references we use no battery, where every KPI equals one, and
the rule-based controller (RBC) supplied with CityLearn. In the ablations, we replace one part of the controller at a
time: fixed weights instead of the tuner (ramp weight 3, carbon weight 1, 24-hour horizon), horizons of 24 and
36 hours, default LightGBM settings, seasonal load and PV forecasts, SAC instead of TQC, and a TQC agent with the same
reward that sets the battery rates directly, with past-only history features and 200{,}000 steps. Ablations with one
seed use seed 42.

\section{Results}\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Performance Across Districts}
The controller improves every KPI in every district (Table~\ref{tab:main}). On buildings 1--5 it lowers the cost by
34.3\% and the emissions by 22.2\% relative to no battery and smooths the district load ($R = 0.823$, $1-L = 0.977$,
$D = 0.900$), whereas the RBC performs worse than idle batteries on every KPI. The gains transfer to the validation
district, where the cost falls by 34.5\% and the emissions by 24.5\%. The scores of the training and validation
districts are almost equal ($S = 0.778$ and 0.771), so the controller is not fitted to the buildings it was trained on.
The seven test buildings differ most from the training ones and gain less ($S = 0.914$), with the cost down by 19.9\%
and the emissions by 4.1\%. The standard deviation across seeds is at most 0.006.

\subsection{Analysis of the Learned Policy}
We first analyse the learned policy through the average daily profile in the training district (Fig.~\ref{fig:daily}).
With no batteries, the district exports up to 5\,kW of PV at midday, which is not paid, and imports about 6\,kW in the
evening. The controller stores this surplus. The mean state of charge rises from under 10\% at dawn to about 87\% at
14:00, and the batteries then discharge through the peak tariff from 16:00 to 21:00 and into the night, which gives a
considerably flatter load at the grid connection. On some days, however, the controller charges from the grid in the
last off-peak hour before the peak tariff. These short import spikes add little to $C$ and $G$ but raise the ramping
and the monthly peak, which is the main reason why $D$ is our weakest KPI.

\subsection{Ablation Study}
Table~\ref{tab:ablation} changes one component at a time. Removing the linear program causes by far the largest drop in
performance: an RL agent with the same reward that sets the battery rates directly reaches only $S = 0.862$ on
buildings 1--5 and a public score of 0.854, even with history features and twice the training, and it ramps more than
idle batteries do ($D = 1.06$). With fixed weights instead of the tuner, the controller scores 0.781 (public) and 0.846
(private) against 0.774 and 0.844, and its Phase~I score rises from 0.717 to 0.742, mainly through cost (0.703 against
0.657).

The benefit of the tuner depends on the forecast accuracy. With seasonal load and PV forecasts, the controller still
performs well in the training district (Phase~I 0.728) but loses most of its advantage on unseen buildings (public
0.807, private 0.864), because the agent overfits its weights to the training buildings. Tuning the LightGBM settings
raises the PV accuracy from about 90\% to 96\% and lowers the public score from 0.782 to 0.774. The horizon and the RL
algorithm have a much smaller effect: horizons of 24, 30 and 36 hours give public scores within 0.002 of each other,
and SAC in place of TQC gives $0.775 \pm 0.001$.

\subsection{Limitations of Load Forecasting}\label{sec:fcvalue}
The load is by far the most difficult quantity to predict. With LightGBM, a single building's hourly load is 50--59\%
predictable (one minus the mean absolute error over the mean load), only slightly better than the seasonal rule. The
district total is more predictable, at 71--78\%, because the random variations of individual buildings partly cancel
out, and this total is what the grid KPIs and the clipped cost see.

To find which errors cost score, we gave the fixed-weight controller the true future for one part of its forecasts at a
time (Fig.~\ref{fig:fcvalue}), which bounds what better forecasts could give. With its own forecasts it scores
$S = 0.784$ in the training and 0.779 in the validation district. Perfect PV forecasts barely change $S$ (0.782 and
0.776), whereas perfect load forecasts lower it to 0.648 in both districts, well below the winner's level. Most of this
value lies in the district total over the next few hours: rescaling the building forecasts to the true total gives
0.649 and 0.648, and the true load for only the next six hours gives 0.659 and 0.651. The performance of the controller
is therefore limited mainly by the accuracy of the hourly district-load forecast, rather than by the solver or the tuner.

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}
In our design, RL is more useful as a tuner of the optimiser than as the controller itself. The forecasts and the linear
program supply the daily patterns of price, PV and demand and the physics of the batteries, and the agent's three
actions only adjust a schedule that already satisfies the battery constraints. The credit-assignment problem over
thousands of hourly battery decisions thus becomes considerably simpler, which explains why removing the solver costs
far more (public 0.854) than removing the tuner (0.781). Two remedies for the weaker grid KPI follow from the analysis: a
load-factor term in the linear program, and better forecasts of the district total, which Section~\ref{sec:fcvalue}
identifies as the main source of error.

Our study has several limitations. It covers one climate and one year of data, with five training buildings. The scores are computed with the official formulas on CityLearn
2.1.2 rather than with the challenge's evaluator, and most ablations use one seed. Beyond these results, the work serves
several of the United Nations Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs), listed in Table~\ref{tab:sdg}.

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}
This work proposed an RL-tuned model predictive controller in which LightGBM forecasts drive a receding-horizon linear
program and a TQC agent retunes its weights every hour. On the CityLearn 2022 benchmark the controller
reduced the electricity cost by 34.3\% and the emissions by 22.2\% in the training district and reached, over three
seeds, a public score of $0.774 \pm 0.003$ and a private score of $0.844 \pm 0.004$, between 7th and 8th place on the
public leaderboard, with the lowest carbon score of all 42 entries. Compared with the winner, the controller achieves a weaker grid KPI, and our analysis shows that this difference stems
mainly from the accuracy of the district-load forecast; with perfect load forecasts the same solver would score about
0.65.
