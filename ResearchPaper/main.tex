\begin{abstract}
Residential batteries can contribute to the reduction of electricity costs and emissions by rescheduling energy
consumption. However, a controller of any battery system in a building is not equipped to be able to predict future
electricity demand, solar generation or carbon intensity. This work proposes a Reinforcement Learning (RL) based model
predictive controller for the batteries of an entire district. The load and solar outputs for every building are
predicted by gradient-boosted forecasters. Using these forecasts, a linear program plans the battery usage for the next
30 hours. An RL agent is trained using Truncated Quantile Critics (TQC) and a reward function based on the official
performance metric. Every hour the agent utilizes the district-level data to compute the three program weights. In the
CityLearn Challenge 2022 benchmark of 17 buildings, the controller reduced the electricity cost by 34.4 percent and
emissions by 22.1 percent in the training district. It achieved a mean public score of 0.774 and private score of 0.843
in Phase III, placing it at the 8th place among 42 public entries. Its carbon score of 0.764 was the lowest among all 42
entries. It is demonstrated through ablation studies that the optimiser, the forecasts and the RL tuning all contribute to the
overall performance.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Buildings account for a large share of total electricity consumption. By installing photovoltaic (PV) panels on rooftops
and home batteries, buildings have been turned into flexible grid resources. Batteries can reduce emission and
electricity costs by charging when carbon intensity and electricity prices are lower. The net demand of electricity from
the grid is stabilized when this is implemented across many buildings across a district
\cite{vazquez2019review,wang2020rlbuildings}. The control problem is an issue as each decision affects the battery's
state of charge and also its performance over the next several hours. Hence, it becomes difficult to determine how much
each action contributed to the final outcome.

Rule-based controllers are simple and therefore struggle to adapt to the weather, the tariff or the state of the grid.
Model predictive control (MPC) plans future actions based on the forecasts. However, its performance highly depends on
the accuracy of its forecasts and on predefined weights that balance electricity costs, emissions and grid impact.
Through multiple interactions with the environment the model-free deep reinforcement learning (RL) \cite{sutton2018rl}
learns a policy \cite{yu2021review,nagy2023tenquestions}. The daily patterns of price and demand, and the long-term
effect of each charge decision are learned from scalar rewards. The CityLearn environment
\cite{vazquez2019citylearn,nweye2024citylearn} and the NeurIPS 2022 CityLearn Challenge \cite{citylearn2022challenge}
provide a common benchmark for this problem. The evaluation score depends on the electricity costs, carbon emissions and
two grid key performance indicators (KPIs).

This work combines the two ideas in an \emph{RL-tuned MPC} (Fig.~\ref{fig:arch}). The electricity demand and production
capacity are predicted by gradient-boosting models. Then, a linear program uses these predictions and generates battery
schedules by taking into account battery constraints. The TQC agent optimizes three weights of the program every hour.
Since the inputs are provided at the district level, the same policy can be applied to districts of different sizes. The
second contribution is a counterfactual reward function which is aligned with the official evaluation metrics. The
function evaluates the difference in metrics in scenarios where batteries are used and not used. The controller is
evaluated on all 17 challenge buildings with the official formulas over five seeds. The results are then compared with
all 42 public leaderboard entries and the private medians. Through ablation experiments the contributions of each
component are evaluated and the forecasting errors with the highest impact on the final score are identified.

\section{Literature Review}\label{sec:related}

\subsection{RL for Building Energy and CityLearn}
V\'azquez-Canteli and Nagy \cite{vazquez2019review} found through most RL studies that simple function approximators,
single buildings and custom simulators were used for demand response and hence made their results difficult to compare.
Later surveys \cite{wang2020rlbuildings,yu2021review} identified sample efficiency, safety, reward design and transfer
across buildings as the main open problems. Nagy \emph{et al.} \cite{nagy2023tenquestions} emphasized the need for shared
benchmarks and KPIs. Multiple problems on real-world RL were listed by Dulac-Arnold \emph{et al.}
\cite{dulacarnold2021challenges}. Among them multi-objective rewards, partial observability and limited data are
applicable to battery control. PV and storage for buildings are provided by CityLearn
\cite{vazquez2019citylearn,nweye2024citylearn}. MARLISA \cite{vazquez2020marlisa} coordinated multiple agents through
sequential action selection. Real-world challenges of multi-agent RL were studied by Nweye \emph{et al.}
\cite{nweye2022realworld} in such communities.

\subsection{Forecast-Driven Optimisation in the CityLearn Challenge}
MPC utilizes forecasting to optimize the decisions in the future but implements the first decision and then repeats the
process \cite{rawlings2017mpc}. All the top entries of the 2022 challenge combined forecasting with optimisation
\cite{nweye2023challenge}. The winner, Together, employed forecasting of the demand for electricity and the PV generation
through gradient-boosted trees and linear models. It then combined a rolling-horizon stochastic optimization method with
multi-agent Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO). In contrast, the runner-up used dynamic programming and a small policy
network trained by CMA-ES. CUFE solved a 24-hour linear program per building, while DivMARL utilized a Soft Actor-Critic
(SAC) policy along with a rule-based system driven by the predictions of XGBoost.

\subsection{Deep RL Algorithms and Reward Design}
Actor-critic methods are the first preference as MPC weights are continuous. Both DDPG \cite{lillicrap2016ddpg} and TD3
\cite{fujimoto2018td3} learn deterministic policies with off-policy critics. SAC \cite{haarnoja2018sac} adds an entropy
term with automatic temperature tuning to encourage exploration. PPO \cite{schulman2017ppo} is a common on-policy
alternative. This study uses TQC \cite{kuznetsov2020tqc}, which extends SAC with distributional critics whose largest
quantiles are removed to reduce over-estimation. TQC is implemented using Stable-Baselines3 \cite{raffin2021sb3}. As
battery control has several conflicting objectives, a single reward needs weights and scales. Ng \emph{et al.}
\cite{ng1999shaping} showed which reward transformations left the optimal policy unchanged, which the proposed counterfactual baseline relies on. Cao \emph{et al.} \cite{cao2020arbitrage} showed that including the battery degradation model in the
reward function changes the learned arbitrage policy. Because deep RL results vary strongly with the random seed
\cite{henderson2018matters,agarwal2021precipice}, the mean and standard deviation over several seeds are reported.

\section{Problem Formulation}\label{sec:problem}

\subsection{Simulation Environment and Observations}
CityLearn 2.1.2 was used with the data from the 2022 challenge. It contains data from 17 buildings across three
different districts: five public training buildings (1--5), five validation buildings (6--10) and seven test buildings
(11--17). Each building consists of a load that it cannot shift, rooftop PV and one battery of 6.4\,kWh and 5\,kW. The
simulation is run on an hourly basis for one year (8{,}759 steps). In each hour the controller decides the charging or
discharging rate of the batteries. Electricity consumption is charged at the time-of-use rate, while the carbon content
of electricity fluctuates throughout the day. The controller checks the calendar, weather along with the 6, 12 and
24-hour forecast of the environment, carbon intensity, price along with its tariff values, and the load, power generated
by PV, state of charge (SoC) and net consumption for each building.

\subsection{Key Performance Indicators}
Let $E_t$ be the district's net consumption at hour $t$ (positive for imports), $\pi_t$ the price and $I_t$ the carbon
intensity. The same quantity in the run without batteries is marked with a superscript ``nb''. The official KPIs are
\begin{align}
  C &= \frac{\sum_t \max(0, E_t \pi_t)}{\sum_t \max(0, E_t^{\mathrm{nb}} \pi_t)}, \qquad
  G = \frac{\sum_t \max(0, E_t I_t)}{\sum_t \max(0, E_t^{\mathrm{nb}} I_t)}, \\
  R &= \frac{\sum_t |E_t - E_{t-1}|}{\sum_t |E_t^{\mathrm{nb}} - E_{t-1}^{\mathrm{nb}}|}, \qquad
  D = \tfrac{1}{2}\bigl(R + (1-L)\bigr),
\end{align}
where $C$ is the cost, $G$ the carbon emissions, $R$ the ramping and $1-L$ the mean monthly value of $1 - \bar E / \max E$
relative to no battery. Exports are not paid. As the clipping applies to the entire district, one building's export can
offset another's import. All KPIs are ratios relative to the no-battery run, hence, values below one are improvements.

The score of a district $d$ is $S_d=(C+G+D)/3$. The first phase of the challenge ranked entries by $(C+G)/2$ on the
training buildings and is called the Phase~I score. The final phase ranked them by a \emph{public} score
$0.4\,S_\mathrm{train} + 0.6\,S_\mathrm{val}$. The winners were chosen by a \emph{private} score
$0.2\,S_\mathrm{train} + 0.3\,S_\mathrm{val} + 0.5\,S_\mathrm{test}$ \cite{nweye2023challenge}. The public score is the main evaluation metric.

Every hour, the controller in Fig.~\ref{fig:arch} makes predictions for the next $H = 30$ hours, and the RL agent assigns
three weights from a district-level state. A linear programming problem, referred to as the solver, uses those weights
to make plans for all batteries over the horizon.

\subsection{Forecasting Module}\label{sec:fc}
For each hour, the loads and PV power generation, the carbon intensity, and the price in the next $H$ hours are forecast.
For each quantity, there is one LightGBM model \cite{ke2017lightgbm} which works at all horizons and buildings. Its
features include recent and lagged values, the average value of the same hour in the past week, the calendar, the current
weather, and the predicted weather of the environment at the target hour. Each value feature is normalized with the mean
absolute value of the same feature of the building in the past week, so that the model that is trained using certain
buildings can be used for others. The district model is trained using the data from the other two districts, and Optuna
\cite{akiba2019optuna} selects the parameters of the model via cross-validation. The carbon intensity is predicted as the
weighted average of the previous day's and the past week's same hour plus the current error with the decay factor of
$0.8^h$, and the price as the tariff of the same hour in the previous week.

\subsection{Receding-Horizon Linear Program}\label{sec:mpc}
The forecast net load of the district $\hat B_h$ (load minus PV), the prices $\hat\pi_h$ and the carbon intensities
$\hat I_h$ are given to the MPC, which then solves
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
\min\; & \sum_{h=1}^{H} \Bigl(\tfrac{\hat\pi_h}{\bar c} + w_g \tfrac{\hat I_h}{\bar g}\Bigr) m_h
        + \tfrac{w_r}{2\bar r} \sum_{h=1}^{H} \rho_h - v\,\eta \sum_b s_{b,H} \\
\text{s.t. } & m_h \ge \hat B_h + \textstyle\sum_b (x^+_{b,h} - x^-_{b,h}),\; m_h \ge 0, \\
& \rho_h \ge |E_h - E_{h-1}|,\; E_h = \hat B_h + \textstyle\sum_b (x^+_{b,h} - x^-_{b,h}), \\
& s_{b,h} = s_{b,h-1} + \eta x^+_{b,h} - x^-_{b,h}/\eta,\; 0 \le s_{b,h} \le 6.4, \\
& 0 \le x^\pm_{b,h} \le \min(5,\ 21 - 3.125\, s_{b,h-1}),
\end{split}
\label{eq:lp}
\end{equation}
where $m_h$ is the district import (exports are not paid), $\rho_h$ the ramp, $s_{b,h}$ the energy stored in battery
$b$, and $x^\pm_{b,h}$ the charge and discharge powers. These powers are limited to 5\,kW, and the efficiency is
$\eta=\sqrt{0.9}$ each way. This final constraint represents the power taper that CityLearn applies once a battery has
passed 80\% of its charge. The running no-battery means $\bar c$, $\bar g$ and $\bar r$ put cost, carbon emissions and
ramping on the scale of the KPIs. $v$ denotes the median cost per unit over the horizon, and it values the energy left at
its end. Only the first hour of the schedule is used. Then the problem is re-solved after another hour
(Fig.~\ref{fig:horizon}). Using HiGHS, each problem is solved in approximately 20\,ms.

\subsection{Reinforcement Learning Weight Tuner}\label{sec:tuner}
Since fixed weights in (\ref{eq:lp}) cannot adjust themselves to seasonal changes, forecast quality, or battery state, an
RL agent produces three scalars $a_t \in [-1,1]^3$ per hour. They set the ramp weight $w_r = 2\cdot 4^{a_1}$, the carbon
weight $w_g = 2^{a_2}$ and the end-of-horizon value $v(1 + a_3/2)$. Each of the weights is centered around a reasonable
fixed value. So a zero action reproduces a fixed-weight MPC. The agent's state contains the forecasts of net load, carbon
intensity and price over the horizon, the time of day and season, the mean state of charge, the current load and month
peak, and the latest forecast error. All of them are independent of the number of buildings. Thus, a policy trained on
five buildings can control the seven test buildings as well.

The agent is trained with a \emph{score-aligned counterfactual reward}. Its terms are built to add up exactly to the
numerators of the official KPIs, on the same scale. The district's clipped cost and emissions and its ramp at hour $t$ are
\begin{equation}
\begin{gathered}
  \hat c_t = \frac{\max(0, E_t\pi_t)}{\bar c^{\,\mathrm{nb}}}, \qquad
  \hat g_t = \frac{\max(0, E_t I_t)}{\bar g^{\,\mathrm{nb}}}, \\
  \hat r_t = \frac{|E_t - E_{t-1}|}{\bar r^{\,\mathrm{nb}}},
\end{gathered}
\end{equation}
where a bar denotes the mean of the term per step in the no-battery run. Hence $\sum_t \hat c_t = T\cdot C$, so the
return over a year is a linear function of the KPIs. The peak term $\hat p_t$ is the rise of the running daily maximum of
$E_t$. The full load is counted in the first hour of each day, so the terms of a day add up to that day's peak. The wear
term is $\hat b_t = \sum_i |\mathrm{SoC}_{i,t}-\mathrm{SoC}_{i,t-1}|$. The reward is
\begin{equation}
  \begin{split}
  r_t = &-\bigl(\hat c_t + w_g\hat g_t + w_r\hat r_t + w_p\hat p_t + w_b\hat b_t\bigr) \\
        &+ \bigl(\hat c^{\,\mathrm{nb}}_t + w_g\hat g^{\,\mathrm{nb}}_t + w_r\hat r^{\,\mathrm{nb}}_t + w_p\hat p^{\,\mathrm{nb}}_t\bigr).
  \end{split}
  \label{eq:reward}
\end{equation}
The second bracket contains the same terms if the batteries were kept idle during that hour. As it depends only on
external data, and not on the actions or the battery's state, it does not affect the optimal policy \cite{ng1999shaping}.
However, it does reduce a major part of the variation caused by demand. In this way, the agent gets the reward for the
improvement it causes, whereas a reward equal to zero would mean ``as good as no battery''. An actual system can calculate
the baseline online based on the measured net load and power of the battery.

The weights are chosen according to the score $S=(C+G+(R+1-L)/2)/3$. The cost gets weight 1, ramping and peak
$w_r = w_p = 0.5$ (peak accounts for the load factor), wear $w_b = 0.1$, and carbon $w_g = 2$. Hence, it puts extra weight
on emissions. The learning algorithm used is TQC \cite{kuznetsov2020tqc}. It contains one actor and two distributional
critics. Both critics have 25 quantiles (from which 2 highest are excluded when the target is formed). This limits the
overestimation caused by a noisy, long-horizon reward.

\section{Experimental Setup}\label{sec:setup}
The tuner is trained for 100{,}000 environment steps or 11.4 years simulated, on the five buildings used for training,
using a learning rate of $3\cdot10^{-4}$, a replay buffer size of 200{,}000 transitions, and a batch size of 256.
$\tau=0.005$, $\gamma=0.99$, 1 gradient step per environment step, 1{,}000 warm-up steps and auto-tuning of entropy are
used in the experiments. The actor and each critic have two hidden layers with 64 nodes each. Five seeds (42, 100, 2026, 7
and 1234) are run and the mean and standard deviation are reported.

This trained policy is run deterministically on each district for the entire year without retraining. Since CityLearn
2.1.2 includes all 17 buildings, the KPIs and public-private score are calculated using the official scoring formulas, and
not the challenge's evaluator. The baselines are no battery, in which case all KPIs will be equal to one, and the
rule-based controller (RBC) provided by CityLearn. For the ablations, one part of the controller is replaced at a time:
fixed weights in place of the tuner (ramp weight 3, carbon weight 1, horizon 24 hours), 24 hours and 36 hours horizon,
default LightGBM hyperparameters, seasonal load and PV predictions, SAC instead of TQC, and a TQC agent with the same
reward that sets the battery rates directly. It uses past-only history and 200{,}000 environment steps. Each RL ablation
is run on three seeds (42, 100 and 2026). The fixed-weight controller has no training and is deterministic.

\section{Results}\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Performance Across Districts}
The controller is evaluated on all three districts in Table~\ref{tab:main}, with every indicator normalised by the
no-battery case. On buildings 1--5, the cost is lowered by 34.4\%, carbon emissions by 22.1\%, and the district load is
smoothed ($R = 0.822$, $1-L = 0.973$, $D = 0.898$). The RBC performs worse than no batteries on every KPI, which shows that
simple rules are not sufficient for this problem. A similar score to training is obtained on the validation district
($S = 0.778$ on training and 0.771 on validation). Therefore, the policy is not overfitted to the buildings on which it
was trained. Fewer improvements are obtained on the seven test buildings ($S = 0.913$, with cost reduced by 20.1\% and
emissions by 4.1\%). The standard deviation across seeds is at most 0.005.

\subsection{Analysis of the Learned Policy}
The learned policy is first analysed through the mean diurnal profile in the training district (Fig.~\ref{fig:daily}).
With no batteries, the district exports up to 5.2\,kW of PV at midday, which is not paid, and imports up to 6.6\,kW in the
evening. The PV surplus is stored in the batteries. The mean state of charge rises from 3.6\% at 06:00 to 86.9\% at 14:00.
The batteries then discharge through the peak tariff from 16:00 to 21:00 and into the night, which gives a flatter load at
the grid connection. However, on some days, the controller charges from the grid in the last off-peak hour before the peak
tariff. These short import spikes add little to $C$ (cost) and $G$ (carbon) but raise the ramping and the monthly peak,
which is the main reason why $D$ is the weakest KPI of the controller.

\subsection{Ablation Study}
Table~\ref{tab:ablation} presents the effect of changing one component at a time. The largest degradation is caused by
the removal of the linear program. When the battery charging and discharging rates are set directly by an RL agent trained
with the same reward, a score of $S = 0.862$ is obtained on buildings 1--5. Only a public score of 0.854 is reached, even
with history features and training steps doubled. Ramping is also increased beyond the no-battery level ($R = 1.15$,
$D = 1.06$). At a 24\,h horizon, the RL weight tuner is compared with fixed weights. With the tuner, public and private
scores of 0.774 and 0.842 are obtained, against 0.781 and 0.846 with fixed weights. The Phase~I score is also improved from
0.742 (fixed weights) to 0.723 (tuner), mainly because the cost is reduced from $C = 0.703$ to $C = 0.666$.

The benefit of the weight tuner depends on the forecast accuracy. With seasonal load and PV forecasts at a 24\,h horizon,
the Phase~I score is improved from 0.744 with fixed weights to 0.732 with the tuner. This gain is obtained mainly through a
lower cost, with $C$ reduced from 0.697 (fixed weights) to 0.670 (tuner). However, it is offset by a weaker grid KPI, with
$D$ increased from 0.934 (fixed weights) to 0.967 (tuner). As a result, no gain in the public score is obtained, with 0.807
(fixed weights) and 0.808 (tuner). The private score is degraded from 0.856 (fixed weights) to 0.864 (tuner). The LightGBM
hyperparameters are then tuned, which raises the PV forecast accuracy from about 90\% to 96\%. The public score is lowered
from 0.782 to 0.774 by this tuning. Much smaller effects are produced by the horizon and the RL algorithm. Public scores
within 0.003 of each other are obtained with horizons of 24, 30 and 36 hours, and a score of $0.775 \pm 0.001$ is obtained
when TQC is replaced by SAC. These differences are comparable to the variation across seeds.

\subsection{Limitations of Load Forecasting}\label{sec:fcvalue}
The load is by far the most difficult quantity to predict. With LightGBM, the hourly load of a single building is 50--59\%
predictable (one minus the mean absolute error divided by the mean load), only slightly above the seasonal rule. The district
total is more predictable, at 71--78\%, because the random variations of individual buildings partly cancel out. This total
determines the grid KPIs and the clipped cost and emissions.

To identify which forecast errors degrade the score, the fixed-weight controller is given the true future values for one part
of its forecasts at a time (Fig.~\ref{fig:fcvalue}). This diagnostic test provides an upper bound on the gain from better
forecasts. With its own forecasts, the controller scores $S = 0.784$ in the training district and $S = 0.779$ in the validation
district. Perfect PV forecasts barely change $S$ (0.782 and 0.776). Perfect load forecasts lower $S$ to 0.648 in both districts,
well below the public score of 0.728 of the first-placed entry. Most of this gain comes from the district total over the next
few hours. Rescaling the building forecasts to the true total gives $S = 0.649$ and 0.648, and the true load for only the next
six hours gives $S = 0.659$ and 0.651. The performance of the controller is therefore limited mainly by the accuracy of the
hourly district-load forecast, rather than by the solver or the tuner.

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}
In the proposed design, RL has more relevance as a tuner of the optimiser rather than as a control mechanism. The predictions along
with the linear program give the daily trends of prices, PV and demand and the physics of the batteries. The agent's three
actions only adjust a schedule that already satisfies the battery constraints. Hence, the credit assignment problem over the
thousands of hourly battery decisions becomes much easier. This also explains why removing the solver is much costlier
(public 0.854) than removing the tuner (0.781). There are two fixes for the weaker grid KPI: a load factor term in the linear
program and improved predictions of the district total that Section~\ref{sec:fcvalue} identifies as the primary source of
error.

One of the limitations of this study is that only one climate zone and one year of data with five training buildings are used.
Also, the KPIs are calculated using the official formula on CityLearn 2.1.2 but not with the challenge evaluator, and each RL
ablation uses only three seeds. Besides the results, the research achieves several United Nations Sustainable Development
Goals (SDGs), listed in Table~\ref{tab:sdg}.

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}
This work presented an RL-tuned model predictive controller where predictions from the LightGBM model drive a
receding-horizon linear programming problem and the TQC agent adjusts its weights every hour. In the CityLearn 2022
challenge, the controller achieved 34.4\% reduction in electricity cost and 22.1\% reduction in the emission in the training
district. Across five seeds, it obtained a public score of $0.774 \pm 0.002$ and a private score of $0.843 \pm 0.003$,
ranking 8th on the public leaderboard. It has the best carbon score among 42 competitors. In contrast to the champion
solution, the controller obtains a worse grid KPI score, and the analysis reveals that this is due mainly to the quality of
prediction of the district load. With perfect load predictions, the same solver will achieve approximately 0.65.
